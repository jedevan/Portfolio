TopicBasic Rules of Differentiation

Activity #4 – Differentiation Rules (20/20)

Description. Ten items where I found f′(x) and named the rule used: constant, power, sum, difference, polynomial function and negative exponent rules.

Reflection. The key idea is that the derivative gives the slope of the function at any point, and the power rule d/dx(xⁿ) = n·xⁿ⁻¹ is the shortcut that makes it practical. Naming the rule beside each answer made me pick the right tool deliberately instead of by habit. The negative exponent items were the trickiest, since I had to subtract one from the exponent, which moves it further from zero. The crossed-out lines on my paper show I corrected my labels while working. Checking each answer against the rule I had named helped me reach a full score.

TopicRoots, Product Rule, Quotient Rule

Activity #5 – Radicals, Product and Quotient Rules (45/45)

Description. Square-root, cube-root and radical items, then three product-rule and three quotient-rule problems, each with u and v written out.

Reflection. The step that unlocked the radicals was rewriting them as powers: √x = x^(1/2), so its derivative is 1/(2√x). For the product rule I listed u and v first, then applied u′v + uv′. For the quotient rule I wrote (vu′ − uv′)/v² and checked the order of subtraction every time, which is where most mistakes happen. Writing u and v separately was my strategy for avoiding errors. I did later find a sign slip on item 8 that my paper did not catch; I explain it in My Growth.

TopicChain Rule

Activity #6 – Chain Rule (20/20)

Description. Four composite functions such as y = (2x + 3)⁵ to differentiate with the chain rule.

Reflection. The chain rule means differentiating the outside function, keeping the inside, then multiplying by the derivative of the inside. For y = (2x + 3)⁵ that gives 5(2x + 3)⁴ · 2 = 10(2x + 3)⁴. My most common mistake was forgetting the inside derivative, so I wrote d/dx of the inside as its own step. On item 4 I got 6(x³ + 2x)⁵(3x² + 2) and wrote “FINAL” twice to remind myself to stop and verify before moving on. The chain rule is the concept I now find most useful for programming, as the IT section shows.

Calculus in IT & Web Development

1. Rate of change → animation and UI motion

The derivative measures how fast a value changes. In a CSS or JavaScript animation, a position is a function of time and its derivative is the speed. Easing curves such as “ease-in-out” are chosen so that speed starts at zero, rises, and falls back to zero, which makes an element glide smoothly instead of jumping. If I understand slopes, I can reason about why an animation feels abrupt.

2. Optimization → machine learning

A derivative equal to zero marks a flat point, which is where a minimum or maximum can occur. Machine learning uses this idea in gradient descent: the program computes the slope of an error function and moves the parameters downhill until the slope is nearly zero. The same thinking applies to minimizing page-load time or server cost.

3. Chain rule → backpropagation

A neural network is a chain of functions, with each layer’s output feeding the next. To learn, it needs the derivative of the final error with respect to early weights. Backpropagation does this by multiplying the derivative of each layer, which is the chain rule applied many times, just like (2x + 3)⁵ in Activity #6.

An incorrect solution and its correction

In Activity #5, item 8, I differentiated y = (x + 1)/x² and wrote y′ = (x + 2)/x³. While reviewing for this portfolio I redid it:

y′ = [x²(1) − (x + 1)(2x)] / x⁴ = (x² − 2x² − 2x)/x⁴ = (−x² − 2x)/x⁴ = −(x + 2)/x³

My original answer lost the negative sign, so the correct derivative is −(x + 2)/x³. The error comes from the order of the quotient rule (v·u′ − u·v′): after subtracting, x² − 2x² is negative. I now check the sign of every quotient-rule answer, and I can explain why a minus sign appears there.

Final Reflection

The most important thing I learned in 2CALC-IT is that a derivative describes how fast something changes, and that the rules are tools for finding that quickly. I started the term thinking I could memorize formulas. My prelim score of 25/50 showed me that memorizing without understanding breaks down under time pressure. The exam sheet is covered in scratch work, which tells me I was trying, but I was not checking my work.

My best-understood topics are the power rule and the chain rule. Activities #4 and #6 were both full scores, and I can explain why 5(2x + 3)⁴ is multiplied by 2. The most challenging topic was the quotient rule, because the order of subtraction matters, and I only found my sign slip in Activity #5 by redoing the problem. My lowest early results, Activity #2 at 76% and the prelims at 50%, were more useful than my perfect scores, because they showed me what to change. I stopped re-reading solutions and started solving from a blank page, writing u and v separately, and naming the rule next to each answer. My midterm of 35/50 (70%) and my 100% scores in Activities #4 to #6 are the evidence.

The infomercial, poster and activity card were tasks where I had to communicate Calculus, not only compute it. Even though I no longer have those hard copies, they changed my attitude: I now try to explain a concept in plain words before I trust that I know it. Early in the semester I saw Calculus as separate from Web Development. Now I see it in animation easing, optimization, and the chain rule behind machine learning, which I expect to meet again in future subjects.

I still need to improve my accuracy under exam conditions, since the midterm showed I still lose points to avoidable slips. My plan is to redo the midterm items I missed, check signs and inside derivatives on every answer, and keep practicing mixed problems. The habits I built here (verifying each step, naming my method, and explaining my reasoning) apply to debugging and to any professional work I do.
